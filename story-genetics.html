Profile — Molecular Genetics

From Mumbai to Molecular Genetics

A No Roadmap Sample Profile · Written to preview the format before real interviews are published

Portrait — Molecular Genetics Researcher

She moved to the United States at fifteen speaking confident classroom English and almost no conversational English, and spent her first year of high school translating jokes half a beat too late. Fifteen years later, she runs a lab studying the genetic mutations behind pediatric neurological disorders — and says the hardest science she ever learned was how to be new somewhere, on purpose, more than once.

Before

In Mumbai, she was the kid who loved Bharatanatyam more than biology — years of classical dance training, recitals, a body that knew a hundred precise gestures before it knew the periodic table. Her parents, both engineers, assumed she'd study engineering too; it was practical, respected, and safe in a way that felt non-negotiable in their household. "Nobody in my family had ever done anything in science that wasn't engineering. It wasn't discouraged, exactly. It just wasn't imagined." At sixteen, if you'd asked her what she'd be doing at thirty, she would have described a slightly different version of her father's career, not her own.

The Detour

Her father's company relocated the family to a suburb outside Chicago the summer before her sophomore year. She describes the first six months less as culture shock and more as a kind of static — she understood every word in her classes and still felt a half-step behind every conversation in the hallway. Her grades, always effortless before, slipped in a way that frightened her. General chemistry very nearly convinced her that science itself, not just engineering, wasn't going to be hers. "I remember thinking: I used to be good at this. Being new took that away from me, and I didn't know if I'd get it back."

The Moment

What got her back into a lab wasn't a discovery — it was glassware. An undergraduate genetics professor mentioned, almost offhand, that his lab always needed someone to wash and autoclave equipment, and she took the unglamorous, unpaid job because it was a door that happened to be open. Six months of cleaning pipettes turned into being handed a real technique to run. A year later, the professor was the one who suggested she apply to PhD programs. "Nobody starts a research career with a discovery. You start by making yourself useful in the room until someone decides to teach you something. I got in through the door nobody else wanted."

Part Four

People assume research is mostly discovery. It's mostly troubleshooting — and it turns out moving countries at fifteen trains you for exactly that. You get very good, very fast, at being lost and staying functional anyway. That turned out to be the actual skill.

She still speaks to her parents in a mix of Marathi and English, still dances occasionally at community events in the town where she now runs her own lab, and still gets asked — usually by well-meaning strangers — how she "ended up" so far from engineering. Her answer has gotten shorter over the years: "I didn't end up here. I found my way here, one badly-labeled test tube at a time."
