The No Roadmap Guide

How to actually start.

Every roadmap ends by telling you to "email a researcher" or "find shadowing." Here's the part nobody hands you: what to actually write, and how to actually ask.

Cold Email Templates

Five situations, five templates. Fill in every bracket — an email that's obviously copy-pasted with brackets left in is worse than not sending one. Click "Copy" to grab the text.

01 — Research Outreach
Subject: Student Interested in Learning More About Research Opportunities Dear Dr. [Last Name], My name is [Your Name], and I am currently a [grade level] at [Your School] in [City, State]. I am deeply interested in [broad interest area, e.g. microbiology], and your research on [specific work they've done, e.g. renal-clearable nanoparticles] caught my attention. [One or two sentences on relevant prior experience — coursework, a project, a competition, anything concrete] Your work in [their overall research area] strongly aligns with my interests, and I am eager to learn more about your research and your journey, gain real-world experience, and contribute in any way I can. If you are able to share information about potential internships, shadowing opportunities, or volunteer roles, I would be truly grateful. My schedule is flexible, and I would greatly appreciate any time you could spare. Thank you so much for your time. I look forward to hearing from you! Warm regards, [Your Name]
02 — Shadowing Request (Physician)
Subject: High School Student Requesting to Shadow Dear Dr. [Last Name], My name is [Your Name], a [grade level] at [Your School]. I'm exploring a career in medicine, and [specialty, e.g. radiology] has stood out to me because [one specific, genuine reason — not "it seems interesting"]. [One sentence on relevant background — a class, a volunteer role, a related interest] I understand shadowing usually has to go through your hospital's volunteer or GME office rather than a physician directly, so if that's the case for you, I'd be grateful if you could point me to the right contact. If you're able to host students yourself, I'd love the chance to observe even a single shift. I'm flexible on timing and happy to work around your schedule. Thank you for considering it — I know physicians get a lot of these requests, and I appreciate you taking the time to read mine. Warm regards, [Your Name] [Phone number or parent/guardian contact, if a minor]
03 — Volunteer Program Coordinator
Subject: Question About the [Program Name] Volunteer Program Dear [Coordinator Name / "Volunteer Services Team"], My name is [Your Name], and I'm a [grade level] at [Your School]. I'm very interested in applying to [Program Name] and wanted to reach out before I do, in case you can answer a couple of quick questions. [Your specific question — e.g. "Is the program open to students outside [City], or only local residents?" / "Is there a waitlist if the summer session is full?"] I've read through the information on your site and I'm ready to apply as soon as the window opens. Thank you for your time — I really appreciate it. Best, [Your Name] [Your email / phone]
04 — Polite Follow-Up (No Response After ~2 Weeks)
Subject: Re: [Your Original Subject Line] Dear Dr. [Last Name], I wanted to follow up on the note I sent on [date] about [research / shadowing] — I know inboxes get busy, so no worries at all if it got buried. I'm still very interested, and my original message is below in case it's easier than searching for it. If now isn't a good time, I completely understand, and I'd appreciate knowing either way so I can plan accordingly. Thank you again for considering it. Warm regards, [Your Name] — [Paste your original email below this line]
05 — Thank You (After a Yes, or After Shadowing)
Subject: Thank You Dear Dr. [Last Name], Thank you so much for [giving me the opportunity to shadow you / agreeing to speak with me / etc.]. [One specific, genuine detail — something you actually learned or noticed, not a generic compliment]. [Optional: a specific follow-up question, or a note on what you plan to do next because of the experience] I really appreciate you taking the time — it meant a lot, and it's genuinely shaped how I'm thinking about [medicine / this specialty / research]. Warm regards, [Your Name]

How To

Finding a Research Lab

Start closer than you think. Look at the faculty directory of the nearest university's biology, chemistry, or medical school department — most list each professor's current research area in a sentence or two. Search their name on Google Scholar or PubMed to see their actual recent papers; referencing a specific one is the single biggest thing that separates a real email from a form letter.

Prioritize labs that have hosted high schoolers before — check the acknowledgments section of a professor's papers, or ask your school counselor which local labs have taken students in past years. A "yes" is far more likely from someone who's done it before.

Asking for Shadowing

Most hospitals route shadowing through a volunteer services or GME (graduate medical education) office rather than letting individual physicians say yes directly — this is a liability and HIPAA issue, not personal reluctance. If a physician doesn't respond, try searching "[hospital name] shadowing policy" before assuming the answer is no.

Be specific about time commitment upfront — "a single shift" is a much easier yes than an open-ended ask. And always have a backup plan: reach out to 8–10 people, not one.

Building a Mentor Relationship

The relationship starts after the first "yes," not before. Send a real thank-you note referencing something specific from the conversation or shadowing day. Follow up every few months with a short, genuine update — not a request — on what you've been doing since. Ask questions that show you've been thinking, not "tell me about your career" but "you mentioned uncertainty is the hardest part — how do you actually sit with that day to day?"

Career Exploration on a Budget

Not every opportunity costs money or requires a plane ticket. Free virtual options: NIH's Office of Intramural Training runs public webinars; most medical schools livestream grand rounds or public lecture series; PubMed and ClinicalTrials.gov are free and searchable by anyone, and just reading five abstracts in a field you're curious about will teach you more about what the work actually looks like than a glossy brochure will.
