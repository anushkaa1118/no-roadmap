Profile — Clinical Ethics

Sometimes Everyone Agrees on the Facts and Still Disagrees on What Should Happen

A No Roadmap Sample Profile · Written to preview the format before real interviews are published

Portrait — Clinical Ethicist

He's not a physician, and he wants that said upfront, because it's usually the first assumption people make about his job. He spent a philosophy degree fielding the question "what job is that supposed to become," and the honest answer took him years longer to find than the question deserved.

Before

Philosophy wasn't a strategic choice — he simply couldn't stop pulling at questions other people treated as settled. It made for good class discussions and a fairly constant stream of relatives asking what, precisely, he intended to do with it. Law school was the working assumption for most of college, mostly because it was the only career anyone could name that seemed to want a philosophy degree.

The Detour

He ended up at a hospital ethics lecture almost by accident, fulfilling a volunteer hour requirement with no expectation of it mattering to his actual path. Sitting in the back of that room, he realized there was a job whose entire function was the thing he'd been doing informally his whole life: sitting inside a disagreement long enough to understand what it was actually about. "Nobody had ever told me this was a profession. I thought I'd have to pick between the thinking I loved and a career that paid for it."

The Moment

An early consult crystallized what the work really was: a family insisting on a treatment the medical team believed was actively harming the patient. His job wasn't to rule in favor of either side. It was to sit with both, asking the same question three or four different ways until people stopped giving their rehearsed position and said what they were actually afraid of. What emerged wasn't a disagreement about medicine at all — it was a fear, unspoken until then, that stopping treatment meant giving up on the person. "Once that was actually said out loud, the whole conversation changed. Nobody needed to be right. They needed to be heard correctly."

Part Four

People assume every hard case in a hospital is a medical mystery. Some of the hardest ones aren't medical at all — everyone already agrees on the facts. They disagree about what those facts mean, and that's a completely different problem to solve.

He still gets asked, gently, whether he ever regrets not going to medical school. He doesn't. "I used to think I had to choose between caring about medicine and being a humanities person. Nobody told me there was a job built for exactly the space in between. I just had to sit in the back of the right lecture."
