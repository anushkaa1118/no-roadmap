Profile — Pediatrics

She Spent Her Childhood Translating. Now She Translates Medicine.

A No Roadmap Sample Profile · Written to preview the format before real interviews are published

Portrait — Pediatrician

By age nine, she was translating bills, school letters, and pharmacy instructions for her parents — carrying a kind of adult responsibility she resented long before she understood it was shaping her. She used to say hospitals made her anxious and medicine was the last career she'd choose. She was wrong on both counts.

Before

Being the household translator wasn't an occasional favor — it was a standing job, one she didn't choose and couldn't opt out of. Doctor's visits were the worst of it: sitting in a small room translating both directions at once, watching her parents' faces for confusion they wouldn't admit to out loud. "I didn't think of it as a skill. I thought of it as exhausting. I remember promising myself I'd pick a career where I'd never have to sit in a room like that again."

The Detour

She avoided anything medicine-adjacent for most of college, until a service requirement placed her at a clinic serving refugee families. She expected to dislike it. Instead, she found herself doing the thing she'd resented as a kid, except now she was good at it on purpose — reading a frightened child's face while a more visibly frightened parent tried to hold it together. "I kept noticing things other volunteers missed. Not because I was smarter. Because I'd had ten years of forced practice."

The Moment

The turn came during a routine appointment, watching a mother nod along as a nurse explained how to use an asthma inhaler for her son. Something about the nodding looked too fast, too automatic — the same nod she remembered giving doctors herself as a translating child who didn't want to admit confusion. She stopped the appointment and asked the mother to demonstrate the inhaler back. The mother couldn't. "That's the moment I understood what I actually wanted to do. Not just explain medicine. Notice when explaining hasn't worked yet."

Part Four

Nobody tells you the child isn't the only patient in the room. The whole family walks in with them — their fear, their confusion, their own history with being believed or dismissed by doctors. You're treating all of it, whether you mean to or not.

She still remembers exactly how it felt to be the nine-year-old translating a diagnosis she barely understood herself. Now she asks every resident she trains to do one thing before ending an appointment: ask the family to explain the plan back, in their own words, out loud. "It's the same thing I wish someone had asked my parents. It just took me twenty years to be the one in the room who could ask it."

Curious about pediatrics or community health?

Start with shadowing at a community clinic — it shows you a different side of medicine than a hospital rotation does.
