Profile — Cardiovascular Perfusion

The Career Hidden Next to the Heart-Lung Machine

A No Roadmap Sample Profile · Written to preview the format before real interviews are published

Portrait — Cardiovascular Perfusionist

He was three years into a physician assistant track before he learned his current job existed. He found it standing in an operating room, staring at someone he couldn't identify, next to a machine he didn't understand — and asking, essentially, "who is that?"

Before

Every career conversation he'd ever had, in high school and well into college, involved the same handful of job titles — doctor, nurse, pharmacist. He was solidly on a physician assistant track, working as an operating room technician partly for experience and partly for tuition money, with no particular expectation that the job would reroute him. "I thought I already knew the full list of people who work in a hospital. I didn't know the list was wrong."

The Detour

During a cardiac case, he noticed someone standing at a machine covered in tubing and monitors, clearly central to the surgery, clearly not the surgeon. Nobody had introduced this role to him in two years of hospital work. He asked afterward, almost as an afterthought, what the job even was. "I remember feeling a little embarrassed asking, like I should already know. Then I found out almost nobody outside the OR knows either."

The Moment

The actual pull came the first time he watched a patient's heart stop mid-surgery, on purpose, and understood in real time that the machine beside him — and the person operating it — was now doing the job of that heart and those lungs. "It's not a supporting role. For however long that surgery takes, that machine and that person are keeping someone alive. I needed to understand exactly how that worked." He finished the PA prerequisites, then pivoted entirely into a perfusion science program instead.

Part Four

High school career fairs show you doctor, nurse, pharmacist, and stop. Hospitals are full of entire careers that never make that list. If you only ever ask the physician what they do, you'll miss half the room.

He still remembers not knowing the word for what he does now until he was twenty-one. It's part of why, when students shadow him, his first instruction has nothing to do with the machine. "Before you watch what I do, go ask everyone else in that room what their job is. I promise you at least one answer will surprise you — it surprised me."
